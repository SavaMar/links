YOU THINK YOU LOOK
PROFESSIONAL ONLINE.
HERE'S WHAT YOUR CLIENTS
ACTUALLY SEE.

Mari Miavka · April 2026 · Moutier, Switzerland

A potential client just Googled your name. Maybe they met you at an event. Maybe someone recommended you. Maybe they saw a comment you left on LinkedIn and thought: interesting.

They opened a new tab. They typed your name.

What did they find?

Most established professionals assume their answer is: "Not bad." A website, a LinkedIn, maybe an Instagram. They have a presence. They show up. They're covered.

They're wrong. And nobody is telling them.

WHAT ACTUALLY
HAPPENS

Let's walk through it. Not from your perspective — from theirs. A stranger who knows nothing about you yet, trying to figure out if you're the real deal.

They land on your LinkedIn. The header image is a blue gradient from a free template. Your headshot is from a conference in 2018 — slightly blurry, slightly cropped at the neck. Your headline says: "[Job Title] at [Company]." Nothing about what you actually do for people. Nothing about what makes you different. Just a title.

They scroll through your posts. The last one was seven months ago. Before that, a share with no comment. Before that, a congratulations post. The message they receive without reading a single word: this person doesn't really show up here.

"The market doesn't judge your work. It judges your presence. And most of the time, those two things are completely different."

They navigate to your website. It loads. It has sections. It says things. But fifteen seconds in, they still can't answer the question they came to answer: is this person good enough for what I need? The photos look like stock photography. The copy sounds like every other professional in your field. There's nothing that says: this is who I actually am.

They click back.

Not because you're not qualified. Because nothing told them you were.

THE FOUR THINGS
THAT CREATE THE GAP

1. The photo doesn't match the level. You've been doing this work for fifteen, twenty years. Your photo looks like it was taken in the parking lot of a hotel during a conference. The quality of your image signals the quality of your work — whether you intend it to or not. A potential client subconsciously matches what they see to what they might pay. If the image says amateur, the price you charge feels high.

2. The visual language is inconsistent. Your website is one aesthetic. Your LinkedIn is another. Your Instagram, if you have one, is a third. They don't connect. There's no through-line that says: this is the same person, this is the same quality, this is the same level. Inconsistency reads as unreliability.

3. The content doesn't build trust before you speak. Your best clients probably came through referrals. They arrived pre-convinced because someone they trusted vouched for you. But a cold contact — someone who found you online — needs to be convinced by what they see before they ever reach out. If your content doesn't do that work, they move on to the next result.

4. Nothing shows the gap between you and everyone else. The thing that makes you genuinely different — your method, your specific expertise, your way of seeing — is invisible. It exists. You know it. Your best clients know it. But a stranger can't see it, because you've never translated it into something visual.

"The gap isn't between you and your competitors. It's between who you actually are and what people see when they find you online."

THE PART NOBODY
WANTS TO HEAR

The professionals who feel this gap most acutely are usually the ones who are best at their work. The mediocre ones don't notice. They have low expectations. They've always looked this way and gotten results that match.

But you — you know something is off. You've felt the frustration of losing a potential client to someone you know is less experienced. You've been in a conversation where you could feel the hesitation, and you had no idea how to address it. You've said your price and immediately started explaining it.

That feeling has a cause. It's not imposter syndrome. It's not a confidence problem. It's a visibility problem. The version of you that exists in the room — capable, experienced, sharp — hasn't been translated into what exists online.

Those are two different people right now. The gap between them is costing you.

THE GOOD NEWS

The gap is fixable. Every single element I described — the photo, the inconsistency, the missing story, the invisible differentiator — is changeable.

But it requires something most professionals resist: seeing yourself honestly from the outside. Not how you feel about your work. Not how your best clients describe you. What a stranger sees in the first fifteen seconds.

That's the hard part. Not the photography session. Not the content strategy. The willingness to look.

Most people wait until they lose enough clients to finally pay attention. A few look before that happens.

If you're reading this, you might be one of the few.
