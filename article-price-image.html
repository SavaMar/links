YOUR PRICE ISN'T
THE PROBLEM.
YOUR IMAGE IS.

Mari Miavka · April 2026 · Moutier, Switzerland

You say your price.

There's a pause. You watch their face.

And then it starts. The sentence that begins: "But of course, that includes..." The careful explanation. The half-apology. The list of justifications you've rehearsed so many times it comes out automatically.

You've been doing this for years. You know you're worth more than you're charging. And still — every time — the number doesn't land the way it should.

I know this feeling. I lived it for a long time. And I want to tell you something that nobody said to me directly when I needed to hear it:

Your price is not the problem.

WHAT HAPPENS
BEFORE YOU SPEAK

By the time you say your price in a conversation, the client has already formed an impression of your value. Not from what you've said. From what they've seen.

They looked you up before the meeting. They formed a view. And that view — based on your photo, your profile, your digital presence — became the frame through which they heard everything that followed.

If that frame says: mid-level professional, hard to distinguish from the others, then your price — even if it's completely fair — will feel high relative to that perception. Not because you charged too much. Because what they saw didn't match what you asked for.

"People don't evaluate price in isolation. They evaluate it against everything else they've seen. And the first thing they saw was you — before you said a word."

The uncomfortable truth: your competitor who charges more isn't more qualified than you. They don't do better work. They've just closed the gap between what they are and what people see. And that perception shift changes the entire pricing conversation.

WHAT "LOOKING PREMIUM"
ACTUALLY MEANS IN PRACTICE

Not expensive. Not over-produced. Not fake.

It means coherent. Your LinkedIn photo, your website header, your Instagram grid — they all feel like the same person. The same level. The same seriousness about their work. When someone encounters you in three different places, they get the same signal. Coherence is read as competence.

It means specific. Your profile says something only you could say. Not "I help clients transform their business." Something that makes the right person stop mid-scroll and think: this person is describing exactly what I need. Generic positioning makes any price feel risky. Specific positioning makes the right price feel obvious.

It means present. You show up. Not every day. Not perfectly. But consistently enough that when someone finds you, there's evidence that you take this seriously. Silence online is its own signal — and it says: this person isn't fully invested. That signal has a price effect.

It means the photo looks like what you charge. I know this sounds shallow. It's not. Visual signals are processed before words, before logic, before the rational part of the brain that evaluates qualifications. A photo from 2017, taken in a hotel corridor, is telling people something about your price before you've said a number.

"Change what people see before you speak. The conversation about price becomes a completely different conversation."

I'VE BEEN ON
BOTH SIDES OF THIS.

For years I was excellent at things nobody could see. I undercharged because — somewhere, below the surface — I believed my visible self matched what I was worth. And my visible self looked like someone who was still building.

When I closed that gap — when what I showed the world finally matched what I knew I was — the pricing conversations changed. Not because I became more confident. Confidence was never the issue. Because the frame people were using to evaluate me changed.

The number stopped needing a defence.

That's the destination. Not "charge more and hope." Charge what you're worth, look like it, and watch the conversation transform.

STOP DEFENDING
YOUR PRICE.

You have two options.

Option one: keep saying your price and immediately explaining it. Keep watching that expression. Keep having conversations where your value is in question before you've said what you do. Keep losing clients to people you know are worse than you, because they look more expensive.

Option two: fix the frame. Invest in what people see before they hear your number. Build a presence that communicates your level before any conversation begins. And then say your price — once, clearly — and let it sit.

You don't need more confidence. You need a presence that earns the number before you say it.

The gap between those two options is not your skill. You've always had the skill. The gap is what people see before they decide.
